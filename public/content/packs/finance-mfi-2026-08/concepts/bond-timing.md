# Dynamic bond funds — flexibility is not foresight

> The pitch: leave the interest-rate calls to the manager. The data: the calls came late.

> [!key] Dynamic bond funds are free to move across maturities — long when rates should fall, short when they should rise — unlike short-, medium- or long-duration funds locked to their bands. The August issue's audit of the recent rate cycle: the **median duration rose only after the 10-year yield had already started falling**, touching over eight years late in the move; the yield bottomed at 6.2% by May 2025. The typical fund **caught part of the rally, but was not early to it**.

## Why this keeps happening

Rate calls are **visibly wrong when wrong** — extending duration early in a falling-rate view that stalls shows up immediately in the NAV. The career-safe move is to follow confirmation, which is precisely what the duration data shows. Freedom in a mandate only pays when a manager will *spend* it before the move is obvious.

> [!more] What to do instead
> If you hold a duration view, the issue's implicit advice is to own it directly (a constant-maturity or target-duration product matches the view you actually have) — or to hold bonds for **carry, not timing**, and stop paying for calls that arrive late. Judge any "flexible" fund by *when* it moved, not whether it eventually did.

## Investor move

- Buying flexibility? **Audit the timing**, not the eventual position.
- Reactive duration = index-like outcomes with active-like fees.
- Own your rate view directly, or own bonds for **carry**.
