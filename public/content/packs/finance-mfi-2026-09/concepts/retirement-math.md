# Retirement math — the number that was never yours

> You passed the test with a corpus figure. The arithmetic still holds; the number you ran it on didn't.

> [!key] Three corrections to retirement arithmetic. **The base**: an Indian retiree must first ask *how much of this money is already promised away* — written claims (a home loan running past retirement) and unwritten ones that "get collected as reliably as a bank's" (a wedding, a parent's care). **The rule**: if the target is 350× monthly expenses, committing a fifth of the corpus lifts it to 350 ÷ (1 − 0.20) ≈ **438×** — the correction rises faster than the share committed. **The shock**: one early hospital bill shrinks income permanently, because early withdrawals cost the most compounding years.

## The corpus rules of thumb

- The simple version: **~25× annual spending** — ₹54 lakh a year calls for **~₹13.5 crore**. An estimate, not a guarantee; aim higher for lifestyle growth or cushion.
- The corrected version: run the multiple on **what's actually yours** — corpus minus promised-away money — not the headline figure.

## The inflation floor and the tax survivor

- **Why you can't just stop**: ₹25,000 a month is ₹3 lakh a year — and next year the same lifestyle costs more. The corpus must out-earn withdrawals *plus* inflation, forever.
- Under the new tax regime, two deductions survived for the salaried: the **₹75,000 standard deduction** (automatic) and the **employer's NPS contribution** — the one worth deliberate attention.

> [!warn] The unwritten promises are the dangerous ones precisely because they never appear in the spreadsheet — the corpus looks funded until the claim arrives. Write them down; fund them separately; then run the multiple.

## Investor move

- List **written and unwritten claims**; subtract before applying any multiple.
- Use **25× annual spending** as the floor estimate; correct upward for committed shares.
- Protect the early retirement years — **shocks then cost the most**.
